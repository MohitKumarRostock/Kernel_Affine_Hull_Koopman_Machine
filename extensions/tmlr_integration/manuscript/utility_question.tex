\section{Predictive usefulness and computational cost}
\label{sec:utility-question}

A forecasting representation can be useful because it retains relevant
information, because it makes that information accessible to a fitted
predictor, or because it reduces the resources needed to achieve a specified
accuracy. These are different properties. Let $X\in\R^d$ denote an observed
history and let $Y\in\R^{d_y}$ denote its future target, where $d$ is the
history dimension and $d_y$ is the number of jointly evaluated target
coordinates. A representation $\Gamma_{\mathcal D}$ is fitted using
development data $\mathcal D$. The complete forecasting procedure also
includes a decoder, regularization, and a model-selection rule. Throughout
this section, expectations are conditional on the fitted state unless
stated otherwise. Each experiment specifies its own evaluation distribution
and loss aggregation; risks from different experiments need not have the
same interpretation or scale.

\paragraph{Information retention and access by a fitted decoder.}
A representation can discard distinctions needed to predict $Y$. It can
also retain those distinctions while making them difficult for a particular
decoder to use. Appending a deterministic representation to the history
produces $(X,\Gamma_{\mathcal D}(X))$, from which $X$ is exactly recoverable.
The augmented input therefore has the same Bayes prediction risk as the raw
history, conditional on the fitted representation
(Proposition~\ref{prop:nf-raw-preservation}). Any improvement from this
augmentation concerns the fitted procedure, including its function class,
regularization, or numerical behavior. Conversely, retaining all information
does not ensure that a finite-sample fitting procedure will generalize as
well as the original procedure.

\paragraph{Predictive structure relative to a baseline.}
Fix a baseline forecast $f_0:\R^d\to\R^{d_y}$ and define its residual by
$e=Y-f_0(X)$. Assume $\E\|e\|_2^2<\infty$, and let
$Z=\Gamma_{\mathcal D}(X)$. For a forecast $f$, write
$R(f)=\E\|Y-f(X)\|_2^2$. Let $P_Z$ be the distribution of $Z$, and let
$L^2(P_Z;\R^{d_y})$ be the class of measurable corrections $q$ satisfying
$\E\|q(Z)\|_2^2<\infty$. The best correction in this class is
$q^*(Z)=\E[e\mid Z]$, and the conditional-expectation projection identity gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:utility-oracle}
 R(f_0)-\inf_{q\in L^2(P_Z;\R^{d_y})}
 R(f_0+q\circ\Gamma_{\mathcal D})
 =\E\|\E[e\mid Z]\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
Here $q\circ\Gamma_{\mathcal D}$ denotes function composition.
Appendix~\ref{app:utility-projection} derives the identity by decomposing the
residual into its conditional mean and an orthogonal remainder. The
representation permits an improvement over this baseline precisely when
the residual conditional mean is nonzero with positive probability.
Unpredictable residual variation does not provide such an improvement,
even when the baseline has a large mean squared error. The oracle class
contains every square-integrable correction, whereas an experiment fits
only a specified finite class. Consequently, Equation~\eqref{eq:utility-oracle}
does not determine the gain of the learned correction.

\paragraph{Accuracy and cost of complete procedures.}
Consider two fitted forecasts $\widehat f_0$ and $\widehat f_s$. Let
$\mathcal E$ denote an evaluation unit, such as an independently generated
episode, and let $\ell(\widehat f;\mathcal E)$ denote the specified average
squared loss of forecast $\widehat f$ on that unit. Their expected improvement is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:utility-delta}
 \Delta_s=\E\!\left[
 \ell(\widehat f_0;\mathcal E)-\ell(\widehat f_s;\mathcal E)
 \mid\mathcal D\right].
\end{equation}
Thus $\Delta_s>0$ favors the richer fitted procedure. A practical comparison
also requires a stated minimum improvement and a resource budget. If these
are $\delta>0$ and $B>0$, respectively, the question is whether the evidence
supports $\Delta_s\geq\delta$ while the complete procedure's cost $C_s$
satisfies $C_s\leq B$. The cost includes feature construction, candidate
fitting, and selection, even when the selected forecast is the baseline.
The evaluation outcomes must be unavailable to both fitting and selection.
Repeated fitting and independently sampled evaluation units quantify
different sources of variability and should not be counted interchangeably.

A complete procedure must also produce a forecast on every declared task.
Even if its candidate class contains the baseline, feature construction or
model search can fail before that candidate is selected. When a task has
neither a prediction nor a prespecified fallback, its loss cannot enter the
planned aggregate. We therefore report fit coverage together with accuracy
and distinguish the full task population from any analysis restricted to
successful fits.

The experiments report error--cost comparisons without imposing a universal
exchange rate between prediction error and computation. An observed
advantage establishes a comparison between the procedures evaluated. It
does not show that all cheaper representations or decoders fail an accuracy
requirement. Such a necessity claim would require a lower bound over a
specified class of alternatives, with the associated sampling and
sensitivity assumptions made explicit.
The empirical comparisons here do not certify minimum feasible
representation complexity or estimate unrestricted Bayes risk on natural
data.

\paragraph{An additive correction that retains the baseline.}
Retaining raw inputs within a larger feature vector need not retain the
original fitted decoder. For example, concatenation changes Gaussian
distances and hence the Nystr\"om basis in the natural-data experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:natural}). An additive construction instead keeps the
baseline forecast fixed:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:utility-additive}
 \widehat f_{g,\gamma}(x)
 =\widehat f_0(x)+\gamma\widehat q_g(x),\qquad 0\leq\gamma\leq1.
\end{equation}
Here $g$ indexes the geometric representation used to fit the correction
$\widehat q_g$, and $\gamma$ is a scalar mixing coefficient. The correction
may use KAHM, affine, or locality features. Setting $\gamma=0$ recovers the
same fitted baseline exactly. Residual fitting is an established method
\citep{Buhlmann2003L2}; our comparison concerns which geometric features
improve it under the stated data and computation budgets.

For fixed $\widehat f_0$ and $\widehat q_g$, assume that the baseline
residual and correction are square-integrable. Expanding squared loss gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:utility-alignment}
 R(\widehat f_{g,\gamma})-R(\widehat f_0)
 =\gamma^2\E\|\widehat q_g(X)\|_2^2
 -2\gamma\E\langle Y-\widehat f_0(X),\widehat q_g(X)\rangle,
\end{equation}
where $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is the Euclidean inner product. For a
correction that is nonzero with positive probability, positive expected
alignment with the baseline residual is necessary and sufficient for some
sufficiently small positive $\gamma$ to improve population risk. The two
expectations in Equation~\eqref{eq:utility-alignment} are unknown during
fitting. A finite candidate grid can miss the improving interval, and a
validation rule can select a worse forecast. Exact baseline recovery is
therefore a property of the candidate class, not a guarantee for the
selected forecast. Appendix~\ref{app:utility-alignment} provides the
corresponding derivation.
