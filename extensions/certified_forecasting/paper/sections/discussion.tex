\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Contribution to KAHM forecasting.}
The procedure combines KAHM reconstruction features with a common observed
prediction target, a constrained decoder class, and calibrated accuracy
decisions. Retaining feature blocks preserves earlier predictors as the
representation grows. The matched study finds consistent KAHM gains on the
nonlinear autoregressive task and against RBF geometry on the fixed PenDigits
cohort. Affine-hull geometry with Frobenius-constrained or rollout decoders is
stronger on the oscillators. The usefulness of KAHM geometry therefore depends
on the task, decoder, and retained resolution. The reconstruction algorithm
is inherited; the certification theory applies to each representation under
its stated assumptions.

\paragraph{Three distinct uses of calibration.}
Validation error ranks fitted candidates. Calibration adds an upper confidence
bound that can certify a population error tolerance, a lower bound that can
exclude that tolerance for an entire sensitivity-constrained decoder class,
and a paired comparison that can certify improvement between two fitted
models. Each answers a different question. Acceptance supports the requested
mean squared error under the evaluation law. Exclusion can motivate changing
the representation or budget. A gap between the bounds can reflect uncertainty
or a loose population obstruction and need not identify a defect in fitting.

\paragraph{Limits of refinement guarantees.}
The method learns reconstruction models and constrained decoders, then selects
among retained dictionary prefixes. It does not prove that geometric clustering
discovers a sufficient state. Residual correlation with a new feature block,
together with unused coefficient budget, gives a sufficient population
condition for one improving direction. Distance expansion gives a necessary
condition for meeting a target accuracy. Neither result guarantees that an
arbitrary split will help. The experiments evaluate fixed candidate families;
sequential refinement with fresh evaluation blocks is a theoretical extension
that was not tested here.

\paragraph{Restricted classes and loose lower bounds.}
Excluding all $\kappa$-Lipschitz decoders of one representation does not exclude
predictors with greater sensitivity, longer histories, or additional observations.
The pairwise obstruction can also be zero when the best risk in the class is
positive. Pairing only at a common origin, for example, can miss information
loss that occurs across origins. A different, class-conditioned pairing law
may yield a more informative bound, but requires its own sampling protocol.
Zero obstruction is therefore neither a certificate of adequacy nor a feature
learning objective in this study.

\paragraph{Independent units and statistical precision.}
Independent episodes are appropriate for independently initialized simulations
and can be appropriate for separate experimental runs. A chronological split
of one long record does not itself supply independent episodes. Extending the
certificate to a dependent series requires quantitative assumptions and a
justified correction for dependence both within calibration and between
training and calibration. An informal autocorrelation estimate or chosen
``effective sample size'' does not supply that correction. The PenDigits study
illustrates another limitation: many strokes and time points can still provide
few defensible independent writer-level units. With so few units, valid
conservative bounds may fail to certify a useful tolerance.

\paragraph{Target bounds and distribution shift.}
The synthetic output ranges follow from the task definitions. PenDigits uses
the acquisition box and a fixed linear scale, without normalizing a completed
trajectory by its future extrema. For unbounded targets, the present
bounded-loss certificate needs a different tail assumption or an explicitly
capped loss; clipping predictions alone does not bound the error. Calibration
under one episode law does not certify performance after changes in dynamics,
noise, acquisition, or origin selection. These requirements apply to all
comparison methods.

\paragraph{Preprocessing as a model choice.}
Training-based range scaling can improve retained soft KAHM forecasts,
including across repeated fits on the fixed PenDigits cohort
(Section~\ref{sec:input-scaling}). The same study finds regressions on other
tasks and with hard assignments. Preprocessing should therefore be an explicit,
validated choice. These results do not justify replacing standardization
throughout the original experiments or assuming that bounded inputs improve
the geometry. Certification after choosing among preprocessing options must
also account for the enlarged family of candidate statements.

\paragraph{Follow-up design and calibration reuse.}
The original-seed preprocessing comparison reuses calibration episodes after
earlier results motivated the follow-up. Its computed endpoints are therefore
nominal summaries; fixing the rerun configuration does not establish independence
from previously observed calibration results. The synthetic confirmation and
matched-geometry experiments use new calibration episodes. Their confidence
statements apply within each declared task and fitting repetition, under the
specified allocation of failure probability. PenDigits follow-ups instead
reuse the previously examined writer cohort. These comparisons are descriptive;
an independent-writer sampling assumption alone does not justify confidence
claims after adapting the study to that cohort.

\paragraph{Experimental and computational scope.}
The study covers controlled dynamics and one real acquisition domain.
The matched comparisons use specified implementations and shared selection
grids; separate tuning for each family could change their ranking. They do
not establish an accuracy frontier against broadly tuned neural forecasters.
Feature dimension measures representation size, not runtime. The prototype
recomputes some shared transforms, so its candidate-level training times do
not provide a fair speed comparison. Archived execution times and environments
document the experiments rather than establish deployment-speed advantages.

\section{Conclusion}

KAHM soft associations can support forecasts of the observed signal and
finite-sample statements about their expected squared error. Retained
dictionaries with a block coefficient budget preserve earlier predictors and
prevent duplication from artificially enlarging the class. The pairwise
obstruction, fitted-risk upper bound, and paired improvement bound distinguish
representation limits from the performance of learned forecasts. The analysis
also supports comparisons with other geometric dictionaries.

The experiments show favorable KAHM comparisons on nonlinear autoregression
and pen trajectories, alongside stronger oscillator alternatives, preprocessing
regressions, and unresolved accuracy decisions. Informative certification
requires credible target bounds, an appropriate evaluation law, and enough
independent observations at the episode level.
