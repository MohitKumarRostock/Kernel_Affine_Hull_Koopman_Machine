\subsection{Writer-Disjoint Forecasting of Original Pen Trajectories}
\label{sec:pendigits}

We evaluate the same forecasting implementations on original PenDigits
trajectories \citep{alpaydin1996pendigits}. This experiment measures performance
on recorded human movement and exposes the distinction between many observations
and many independent sampling units. It is not evidence that the dataset
satisfies the independent-episode assumption of our population-risk theorem.

\paragraph{Data and causal inputs.}
The UCI archive contains unnormalized, time-sampled pen locations as well as a
commonly used table of eight spatially resampled locations per digit. We use the
original UNIPEN records. The table's normalization and resampling depend on the
completed character, so we do not use it for causal forecasting. The acquisition
protocol specifies coordinates in $[0,500]^2$ and a nominal $100$ ms sample
interval. We use the fixed transformation $x\mapsto x/250-1$, learned predictors
are clipped to $[-1,1]$, and every source coordinate is checked against the
declared acquisition range. There is no per-stroke centering, alignment,
normalization, or resampling.

The documented source split contains 7,494 digit records from 30 development
writers and 3,498 records from 14 different test writers. We infer the writer
identifier from the second integer in each UNIPEN \texttt{COMMENT} field: its
range and approximately 250 records per identifier match the documented writer
counts. The source text does not explicitly define these comment fields, and
this inference is recorded in the data provenance. Identifiers are qualified by
the source split. Pen-up gaps are not treated as ordinary time steps.

We retain the first pen-down stroke of a digit only when it contains at least
32 samples, and use its first 32 points. This leaves 1,751 test digits from 13
eligible test writers; one of the original 14 writers has no qualifying first
stroke. The population of interest in this experiment is therefore restricted
to the stated eligible-stroke protocol and writers with at least one eligible
stroke. Eligibility depends on the completed stroke length, so this is a retrospective eligible-stroke task, not a guarantee for every newly started online stroke. No loss is assigned to the excluded writer. The initial run's check for
14 eligible writers failed before any test predictions were evaluated; that
failure and the corrected 13-writer accounting are retained in the release.

\paragraph{Development and evaluation.}
Within the original development file, the first 24 writer identifiers are used
for fitting and the remaining six for validation. Each of three fixed seeds
selects 20 eligible strokes per fitting writer, for 480 fitting strokes. All 717
eligible strokes from the validation writers are used for selection. The two
coordinates are modeled separately from their own scalar histories. Histories
have lengths 4 or 8; forecast horizons are 1, 2, and 4 samples. The common
zero-based forecast origins are $7,11,15,19,23,27$. Thus every model predicts
the same targets from available past observations.

For each coordinate and seed, the candidate family contains 40 models: the
three soft geometric feature families at four cumulative stages and two history
lengths, the KAHM ablations and Koopman rollouts, autoregressive and quadratic
baselines, persistence, and a constant. Geometric block sizes are 2, 4, 8, and
16; other settings are $\kappa=4$, ridge coefficient $10^{-4}$, and affinity
exponent 16. One model per family is selected by its mean validation-writer
squared error averaged over the three horizons. All six coordinate/seed
families and their selections are frozen before test scoring. Every fitted
candidate is retained, including those not selected for the table.

Within each writer, we average loss over origins and then eligible digits;
the cohort loss gives each eligible writer equal weight. Table~\ref{tab:pendigits}
pools the two coordinate MSEs, takes their square root, and reports the mean and
sample standard deviation over the three development seeds. These standard
deviations measure sensitivity to fitting randomness on the same test cohort;
they are not confidence intervals over independent test cohorts. A normalized
RMSE of 0.01 corresponds to 2.5 acquisition pixels.

\paragraph{Findings and limits.}
Quadratic regression is the most accurate reported family at all three
horizons. Its mean RMSEs are 0.00768, 0.01777, and 0.05184, compared with
0.04661, 0.06686, and 0.11269 for the KAHM block predictor. Ridge
autoregression also outperforms the KAHM block predictor. Relative to
persistence, KAHM is worse at one step and better at two and four steps.
The result does not support general forecasting superiority of KAHM geometry.
The nested-block and ordinary Frobenius fits are effectively identical here
because the selected fitted coefficients are feasible under both budgets;
different constraint definitions need not change a particular optimum.

For completeness, we calculate an upper bound for every scalar-coordinate,
seed, candidate, and horizon under the explicit additional assumption that the
eligible writer records are iid from a common writer population and independent
of the development records. The data documentation does not establish that
sampling assumption. Each writer-aggregated scalar loss is in $[0,4]$. A joint
failure budget of $0.05$ over $2\cdot3\cdot40\cdot3=720$ statements gives the
predeclared Hoeffding addition
\[
  4\sqrt{\frac{\log(720/0.05)}{2\cdot13}}=2.42741
\]
to empirical MSE, clipped at 4. The corresponding bounds on coordinate-pooled
RMSE are approximately 1.56, much larger than the observed errors and any
reported tolerance of 0.2, 0.35, or 0.5. None of these useful tolerances is
certified. We retain this outcome: 1,751 digit trajectories and thousands of
overlapping prediction windows do not supply 1,751 independent writers.
The real-data experiment therefore provides descriptive evidence about
writer-disjoint forecasting and a concrete limitation of generic population
certification with few independent groups.

The release includes the official download URL and archive hash, the parser,
the length-filter accounting, all training record identifiers, the frozen model
descriptions, and all 9,360 candidate--writer--horizon losses. Recomputing the
reported cohort MSEs from the exported loss table agrees to within
$1.4\times10^{-17}$. All recorded block-norm budgets are feasible and all
reported optimization gaps are below $10^{-7}$. These checks verify the
reported numerical computation; they do not establish the iid-writer assumption.
