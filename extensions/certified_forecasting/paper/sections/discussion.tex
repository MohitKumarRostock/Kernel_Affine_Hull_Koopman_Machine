\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What a lower certificate adds.}
An upper certificate establishes that a fitted forecast meets a tolerance.
A lower certificate can exclude every decoder in a specified sensitivity
class. Together they can identify the minimum feasible representation cost
among predefined alternatives, even when upper-only selection returns the
same predictor. The learned-KAHM decision study connects this distinction to
fitted geometric representations on fresh independent episodes. Its
constructed mechanism isolates a recoverable forecasting signal and an
independent-future control; it does not
establish how often such decisions can be resolved on naturally acquired
data. Costs refer to the declared feature counts, not globally optimal
forecasting complexity or runtime.

\paragraph{Population slack and calibration precision.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:witness-slack} identifies two sources of pair-witness
slack: a nonzero conditional residual mean and a loose geometric inequality
for paired residuals. The block hierarchy supplies a systematic route beyond
the information in individual pairs. Its monotonicity concerns population
expectations. Convergence to the common decoder's optimal risk requires
unconditional iid draws, bounded scalar targets, and compact representation
support. Conditional blocks instead approach an average of conditional oracle
risks, which can be smaller than the best risk of one shared decoder. A larger
block leaves fewer
independent scores and costs more to optimize. It can therefore have a weaker
finite-sample endpoint despite a larger population expectation. The
empirical-minimum comparator remains an important alternative; the hierarchy
does not establish universal statistical or computational superiority. The
block benchmark studies finite-support distributions, while the learned-KAHM
decision study uses stratified pair certificates. The latter therefore tests
class exclusion with learned geometry, rather than a finite-sample advantage
for larger blocks on that geometry.

Stratification addresses a different issue by changing which observations
are compared while preserving the marginal risk. It can recover rare
obstructions, but it can also remove informative comparisons or leave too
few observations in a stratum. Estimated masses add uncertainty. Neither a
zero pair obstruction nor an unresolved block certificate establishes
representation adequacy. Furthermore, a sharp bound for the full Lipschitz
class can remain loose for a restricted affine decoder family.

\paragraph{What refinement guarantees.}
Appending feature blocks preserves earlier feasible forecasts. Exact
duplication leaves the block-norm class unchanged, although it can change a
ridge-regularized fit. These class properties do not force independently
fitted predictors to improve. Residual correlation supplies a sufficient
improvement condition when coefficient budget remains available; a separate
result gives a necessary representation-distance expansion for a requested
tolerance. Neither result guarantees that unsupervised clustering discovers
a sufficient state. The experiments freeze their candidate families before
calibration. Sequential refinement using fresh evaluation blocks remains
untested.

\paragraph{Sampling populations and adaptation.}
Synthetic episodes are independently generated. PenDigits supplies only 13
eligible held-out writer identifiers, inferred from source fields; its
generic endpoints are uninformative. Speech supplies documented recording
identifiers and 978 calibration groups, permitting informative upper bounds
under the explicit iid group-record assumption. Source identifiers and
disjoint splits do not establish random population sampling. Eligibility
rules based on completed recordings and source quality screening further
restrict both real-data populations. Repeated fitting seeds on the same
cohort measure fitting variation, not independent population replications.

Target ranges follow from the synthetic laws, the PenDigits acquisition box,
or the fixed speech-energy transformation. Clipping predictions cannot bound
loss for an unbounded target. The certificates do not transfer across
changes in dynamics, sensors, noise, or origin laws. Original-seed scaling
follow-ups reuse previously examined calibration, and their endpoints are
nominal. Fresh synthetic studies use the stated independent streams;
PenDigits and speech scaling follow-ups on examined cohorts remain
descriptive. Confidence allocations cover each declared study, not the
entire research campaign.

\paragraph{Accuracy and preprocessing.}
The matched comparisons support KAHM gains on nonlinear autoregression and
against RBF geometry on the fixed PenDigits cohort. Affine-hull alternatives
are stronger on the oscillators, while autoregressive, quadratic, and kernel
regressors outperform KAHM on speech. Scaling to $[-1,1]$ improves some tasks
and worsens others. It must be treated as a development-selected model choice,
with training extrema frozen before calibration; clipping future inputs is
a distinct representation. These experiments assess the stated certificate
framework and forecasting implementations. They establish neither universal
KAHM superiority nor an accuracy frontier against broadly tuned neural
forecasters. Execution times depend on hardware and implementation, and
feature count alone does not determine computational cost.

\section{Conclusion}

Pairwise obstructions are the first member of a hierarchy of class-wide risk
lower bounds. Larger blocks reduce population slack under the stated
conditions, while calibration exposes the cost of estimating those bounds
from finite independent data. Conditional pairing and retained feature blocks
provide complementary control over representation comparisons. Together with
fitted-risk upper bounds, these tools can certify achievable accuracy,
exclude inadequate classes, and resolve a declared minimum-cost decision.
The learned-KAHM study demonstrates that mechanism in a controlled setting;
the broader experiments identify task-dependent accuracy, scaling effects,
and unresolved decisions that remain part of the method's scope.
